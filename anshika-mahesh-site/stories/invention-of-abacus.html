Chapter 1

The Dead Phone Project

Samyuktha and Anshika were in their room doing a project about the abacus. They were preparing a speech and writing an essay about it. But except for the fact that it was used for maths, they didn’t know anything else about it.

Their phone was dead too, so they couldn’t research.

Just as they were starting to worry…

Chapter 2

Magic Dust to China

…magic dust surrounded them, and before they knew what was going on, they were in China!

The air smelled different. Lanterns glowed. A wooden building with a curved roof stood ahead — a museum.

“Did we just—?” whispered Samyuktha.

“Don’t pinch too hard,” said Anshika. “If this is a dream, I still want the facts for our essay.”

They walked inside.

Chapter 3

At the Museum

They saw displays about counting tools and the famous Chinese abacus — the suanpan — with neat rows of beads on rods.

A board said the abacus was used for maths long, long ago. One label mentioned times around 500 BC — so ancient people were already finding clever ways to count!

But the girls frowned. Next to “Inventor” the space seemed… empty of one neat famous name.

“His name was…?” Anshika began.

“Born on…?” Samyuktha tried.

They didn’t know. Their project blanks were still blank — until a girl approached them.

Chapter 4

My Name Is Mimi Cheng!

Looking at them, a girl approached and said, “Hello! My name is Mira — but everyone here calls me Mimi Cheng. Can I help you?”

“Hello, my name is Anshika, and this is Samyuktha,” said Anshika. “Yes — we would like to know more about abac—”

“Abacuses!” finished Samyuktha. “For a school project. Our phone died. Then… magic dust.”

Mimi’s eyes lit up. “Then you came to the right place. Follow me.”

She led them to a big wooden abacus under soft museum lights.

What Mimi told them (the honest bit): There isn’t one single famous inventor with a birthday on a neat plaque. Counting boards and bead frames grew over many years in different places. In China, the suanpan became a beloved tool — and people still learn it today.
Chapter 5

Beads That Count

“Watch,” said Mimi. She slid beads with quick fingers. “Each rod is a place — ones, tens, hundreds… Move the beads, and you can add and subtract without writing every number.”

Anshika tried. Click-click. Samyuktha tried. Click-click-click.

“It’s like a calculator made of wood!” said Samyuktha.

“A calculator that doesn’t need charging,” grinned Anshika. “Unlike our phone.”

Mimi taught them enough for a speech: what an abacus is, that it helped with maths for centuries, that China has a famous style with beads on rods, and that you learn by practising with your hands — not only by memorising a date.

“For your essay,” Mimi added, “write that people invented better ways to count because they needed them — for markets, building, and school. The abacus is one brilliant answer.”

Chapter 6

Home Again

“Thank you, Mimi Cheng!” said both girls together.

Golden dust swirled again. POOF — they were back in their room. The phone was still dead. The essay page was still waiting.

But now they had plenty to say.

They wrote about beads and rods, about ancient counting, about China, and about how a tool can be invented by many clever hands over time — not always by one person with a birthday.

Their speech went well. Their teacher smiled. And whenever Anshika and Samyuktha saw an abacus after that, they remembered Mimi’s quick fingers — and the click-click of magic that started with a dead phone.

The End
